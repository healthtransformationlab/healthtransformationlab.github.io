PerioBench

Definitions, sources and orders

Three choices decide whether a record counts as gum disease. Each one changes the answer.

Synthetic data. Every number here comes from synthetic records, so it shows how the choices differ, not how often each is right on real patients (how scoring works). The full, regularly regenerated page: docs/standards.md.

  1. The definition: what counts as gum disease, and how bad.
  2. The sources: which parts of the record to read.
  3. The order: which source to believe when they disagree.

1. The definitions

DefinitionMade forAnswers
2017 AAP/EFPPatient care: stage, grade and extenthealthy, gingivitis, stage I–IV
2012 CDC/AAPCounting gum disease in populations (the NHANES survey)none, mild, moderate, severe
ACESResearch on chart data: the 2017 system from the chart aloneas 2017

Where they disagree, on the same synthetic visits. These use all 3,437 synthetic visits with a gum chart (out of 4,343), not the 240 test charts the methods are scored on, because the definitions need only the chart:

  • Every stage I visit is "none" under CDC/AAP, because CDC/AAP needs at least 3 mm of attachment loss on two teeth. So the 2017 system counts more people as having gum disease, and prevalence from the two can't be compared directly.
  • ACES agrees with the 2017 worksheet on 3,210 of the 3,437 charted visits. It moves some stage III to II, and some stage IV to III.

2. The sources

A dental record holds several parts that each say something about gum disease: the gum measurements (probing chart), the clinician's note, the exam form, diagnosis codes, procedure codes and an X-ray reading. How each is turned into a status: evidence rules.

How often they agree (kappa: 1 means always, 0 means no better than chance):

PairAgreeKappa
Chart note and gum measurements78%0.72
Exam form and gum measurements74%0.68
Chart note and exam form63%0.53
Procedure codes and X-ray reading (least)61%0.20

On synthetic records the generator decides how often the parts disagree, so these show the kind of disagreement, not its real size.

3. The orders

When the parts disagree, a rule set believes the first one, in a fixed order, that gives an answer.

OrderBelieves first … lastTested against experts?
Patel et al. 2022 (paper)the clinician's recorded diagnosis → notes → gum chart; each only fills gapsNo: only parts were checked, not the order
The study design'sexam form → diagnosis codes → note → gum measurements (adds diagnosis codes to Patel)Not yet
This project's placeholdergum measurements → note → codes → exam form; the X-ray can only raise the stageNot yet

On 240 synthetic charts, measurements first agrees with the answer key on 234 and exam form first on 173. That flatters measurements first, because the answer key was made from the measurements. On real records, which order is best is an open question.

4. Decisions still open

  • Keep the diagnosis-codes step (the paper has none), and whether planned treatment counts.
  • Which order to use on real records, chosen with real reviewers.
  • Whether procedure codes stay out and the X-ray reading comes in.
  • Report prevalence under CDC/AAP next to 2017, to compare with national figures.
